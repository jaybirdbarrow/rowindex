AI

AI is moving faster than any technology before it, and it's already changing how people work, talk to each other, and think about what their lives are for. There's no precedent for regulating something that reaches this deep into daily life, this fast.
1 indexed
5 outcome lines
Edition 1 · September 2026
01

The snapshot

AI went from research project to global infrastructure in about three years, and the upside is already real: faster drug discovery, tutoring that adapts to one kid instead of thirty, code and analysis that used to take a team a week done in an afternoon. Nobody serious wants to slow that down for its own sake.

The same speed cuts the other way. The systems keep getting more capable before our institutions can fully digest them. The U.S. has leaned on voluntary commitments while individual states pass their own laws, and labs test their own systems and publish the results, which is better than nothing but not the independent oversight we need.

The technology moves fast. The rules don't keep up. That gap is what this arena is about: capturing the upside without losing control of the downside. Everything below is aimed at closing it.

Climate

Climate conversations can miss the energy trilemma: energy has to be clean, reliable, and affordable at the same time, and pushing hard on one often costs you the other two. That tension is what makes this work harder than it looks from the outside.
1 indexed
5 outcome lines
Edition 1 · September 2026
01

The snapshot

More solar came online globally last year than every other energy source combined, and clean electricity is now the cheapest electricity in most of the world. Building the clean power itself is going well, but wiring it into the grid gets less attention. Our infrastructure is built on oil and gas, and transitioning will take time and tough choices.

Reliable and affordable are lagging behind. Most of the grid carrying that power was built for a different era. Policy support has also gotten less predictable, which makes the multi-year bets this work requires harder to place.

Everything below is aimed at moving clean, reliable, and affordable closer together instead of trading one for another.

Democracy

This arena boils down to defending what exists today and building something more resilient.

01

The snapshot

We focus on a few critical mechanics: elections that produce results people accept, checks that keep any single branch from running unchecked, a Congress that can get legislation passed, and reining in money in politics.

Some of the work is protecting dynamics that already function: courts still ruling, agencies still enforcing the law, elections still getting certified.

The rest is building versions of those mechanics that hold up better under pressure than the current ones do.

The larger project is keeping a two-hundred-year-old system flexible enough to bend without breaking. Rules written for a slower era have to adapt to a faster one, and stay trustworthy along the way.

Culture

This is a values arena: whether people get to live healthy, wealthy, rewarding lives, and whether the dynamics working against that get better or worse. The bigger test is whether we can digest fast technological and economic change in a way that leaves people more social and more fulfilled, not less.

The snapshot

The downstream effects of a disconnected, squeezed culture show up everywhere: polarization, mental health strain, falling birth rates, people who can't quite say why they're not thriving. I picked the dynamics on this page because they felt load-bearing: meaning if we solve one, the pressure on the rest eases up.

None of this has a clean legislative fix. Nobody passes a bill that makes people feel less alone or more hopeful about their kids' future. The people working this arena are building better versions of community, care, and meaning, brick by brick.

Everything below is that shortlist: the handful of things that, if they get better, make the rest of a good life more possible.

Method

Our institutions were built for a different century. The teams in this arena are building the replacements.
4 indexed
3 outcome lines
Edition 1 · September 2026
01

The snapshot

Government agencies, universities, and big foundations were built for a slower, more predictable century, and the pace of change is outrunning their ability to keep up.

That's the problem underneath all the others: how we solve problems matters as much as what we're solving. The people inside these institutions are usually excellent. What limits them is the shape of the institution itself, how it's funded, how fast it can move, and what it's legally allowed to do. The work here is inventing better shapes: new legal structures, new funding models, new ways to move money and talent, instead of defaulting to the traditional nonprofit playbook every time.

Emerson Collective

Arena 06 · Method
A new take on philanthropy, built as a full-stack operating institution.
A flexible and durable structure that grants, invests and operates across the societal problem space.
LLC Indexed
The induction · 90s

Why the seat

Billionaires have come under fire lately, and I get the argument. Emerson is on this list because it's one of the best examples I've found of a high-net-worth person choosing to work these problems at a different altitude. The traditional playbook is a private foundation: stock goes in, they get the tax deduction, and a small percentage of the money is actually deployed to solution work. I can't knock that path entirely, but my sense is it moves too slowly to meet the moment we're in.

Laurene Powell Jobs built Emerson to deploy. It's an LLC with a nonprofit arm, so it can grant, invest, own, run, and advocate, and it picks whichever one moves the problem fastest. The bigger difference, though, is the operating posture. Most philanthropy strokes the check and steps back. Emerson often builds the thing, then puts an operator inside it to run it and keeps them on the hook for whether it works. Speed and impact over the long, safe drip. It's the model I'd point any new fortune at.

Helena is the organization that first got me excited about the people driving societal progress and the technologies underneath them. The membership is the other thing that sets them apart. More than a hundred experts across disciplines, in a society that keeps them siloed everywhere else, and the results speak for themselves. And they look and feel like the leader they are: clean brand, deep-dive content, and nuance running through all of the work.

Stripe

Arena 06 · Method
A new, durable brand of corporate social responsibility.
Stripe is setting the standard for how a company engages big problems, with a public goods arm that builds markets and operates solutions.
Public benefit LLC (Stripe subsidiary) Indexed
The induction · 90s

Why the seat

Stripe wasn't the first to set up an advance market commitment, but it did it the loudest. When they stood up Frontier to build a market for carbon removal, the headlines were deserved. What strikes me is what happened after. ESG and corporate social responsibility faded across the industry, and Stripe stayed the course and pushed further, growing the effort into what they now call Public Goods. The fundamentals of Frontier are simple: get a group of companies to promise, together, to buy carbon removal before it exists. A guaranteed customer is what lets a startup borrow the money to build the plant.

They're now running the same play on respiratory infections. Intercept launched in June with $500 million alongside Anthropic, the OpenAI Foundation and Flu Lab, with the mission of making the common cold and flu a thing of the past. The founders seem to care about building something that actually works, and they've stayed at it long enough to prove it isn't performative. Stripe is the best model for a company truly operating a solution vs. donating to one and just hoping for the best.

About row99

The top 99 teams fixing society's hardest problems.

row is building an index of the teams doing the world's most consequential work so talent and funding know where to go.

01

why the index exists

If you want to know how the economy is doing, you look at the S&P 500. If you want to know who's playing good ball, you look at a league's standings. Restaurants have Michelin, movies have the box office.

Society's hardest problems have no such thing.

And the way most of us hear about these big problems hands us the downside with nothing to do with it. It's easy to know the climate is in trouble, hard to know the key players doing the best work to fix it. Same for AI, same for democracy. That gap is where a lot of the dread comes from, and I believe it's a fixable gap.

It shouldn't be hard to name the five best teams on AI safety. Or the grid. Or mental health. Or getting money out of politics. The information is out there, but it's scattered and quiet, making it tough for a normal person to engage with.

I think this is a huge miss. Partly because it keeps these problems and teams from being broadly legible, and partly because engaging with these organizations tends to inspire the kind of optimism and creativity we need more of.

02

what it is

The inaugural row99 has 99 seats across six arenas.

Each seat goes to one team fixing one of society's hardest problems. Right now that's AI, climate, democracy, culture, information, and what I'm calling "method," teams changing how we solve problems.

As the index grows, so does our picture of a field. "The people working on democracy" becomes something tangible. You can navigate it, compare teams, and argue about who should be in there. That argument is the sign it's working, btw.

03

how the list gets made

Right now it's just one guy's read. I research the players, look at what the work has produced, and make selections. For every seat, I do a 90-second explainer on who they are, why they're distinctive, and the outcomes they're driving.

For society to get its sh*t together on its hardest problems, we need a shared space to understand the problems and the key players. Row is building it.

The standard

What earns a seat.

99 seats, filled one defense at a time. Here's the bar, so you can hold me to it.

1

The work moves an outcome line

Every seat attaches to at least one thing that has to become true. If I can't name the line an org moves, they don't get a seat — however famous they are.

2

There's a receipt

A specific, dated thing they did. A mission statement is a plan; a receipt is proof. Every profile carries at least one.

3

The work would be missed

If they stopped tomorrow, someone working this problem would feel it within a year. That's the difference between leading work and adjacent work.

4

The seat doesn't ask your tax status

Companies, nonprofits, funders, media — the grid holds all of them, because the problems don't care about corporate structure and the leading work is wherever it is.

5

I publish the defense

Every seat comes with my reasoning, in writing, with my name on it. When I get one wrong, the correction ships with the same volume as the pick.

The payer rule: no organization on the list pays to be on it, and none can. Readers and researchers pay for depth. The people being judged never pay the judge — that's the whole reason you can trust the grid.
